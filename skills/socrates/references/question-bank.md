# Question bank

Questions Socrates can reach for by situation. None of these is required.
They are here for the moment when the right question does not come.

Every question here is one question. Ask one per turn.

## Openers

Purpose before content. The first question should make the student say what
the reading is *for*.

- What is the author trying to convince you of?
- Why did the author think this needed writing? What was wrong before it?
- If this reading is right, what should someone do differently?
- Who is the author arguing against, even if they never name them?
- What question is this reading an answer to?

## Probing the claim

- Say that again as if to a friend who has not read it.
- Is that what the author claims, or what you expected the author to claim?
- Where does the author say that? Page?
- Which word in that sentence is doing the most work?
- If the author had to give up one sentence from the whole piece, which one
  would hurt the argument most?

## Probing the support

- What would have to be true for that claim to hold?
- What does the author offer as evidence for that? Is it evidence or
  illustration?
- How does the author know that? Did they measure it, argue it, or assume it?
- What would the author accept as showing they were wrong?
- Is that the author's finding or the author's interpretation of the finding?

## Elenctic moves

Use when the student is wrong. Find the agreed point first.

- A minute ago you said X. Now you are saying Y. Can both be true?
- The author spends a whole section on Z. Does an author who believes what
  you just said write that section?
- You agree the author accepts A. Does A fit with your reading that the author
  believes B?
- Suppose the author read your summary. Which sentence would they object to?
- You said the evidence shows X. You also said the sample was only Y. How does
  one get from Y to X?

## Probing a definition or distinction

- The author uses the word W. Does it mean what it means on the street?
- What is the difference the author is drawing between P and Q? Give me an
  example of each.
- Could the argument be made without that distinction? What would break?

## When the student quotes

- Good. Now what does that mean?
- Why that sentence? What does it do for the argument?
- Put the author's sentence away. Say it.

## When the student is stuck

Narrow, don't rescue.

- Go to page N, second paragraph. Read it, then tell me what it says.
- Forget the whole piece. What is section 3 about?
- What is the one thing you are sure the author believes?
- What did you expect the author to say that they did not?

## When the student asks Socrates to explain

Decline and turn it around.

- What do you think the author would say if you asked them that?
- You have the text and I have questions. Where would you look?
- Make a guess. We can test it.

## When the student objects to the author

Welcome it, strengthen it, test it.

- Good. Say the objection as strongly as you can.
- Did the author see that coming? Where do they deal with it?
- What would the author say back?
- Is your objection to the claim, the evidence, or the inference between them?

## When the student agrees with everything

- Where is the author weakest?
- What is the claim you would least want to defend in front of a skeptic?
- What did the author leave out that a critic would bring up first?

## When the student dismisses everything

- What is the strongest thing in here?
- If a smart person believes this, why?
- Which part of it survives your objection?

## Closers

- You have the pieces. Give me the reading in three to five sentences of your
  own words. Cover the claim, what it rests on, and what you make of it.
- That is the author's abstract rearranged. Say it as you would say it.
- Your summary says what the author claims. What do you think of the claim?
- Your summary says what you think. Where is the author's evidence in it?
- Read your own summary back. Which sentence would you cut?
